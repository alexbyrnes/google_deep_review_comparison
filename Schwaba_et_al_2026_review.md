# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY
---
## Summary
This manuscript reports a comprehensive genome-wide association study (GWAS) meta-analysis of the Big Five personality traits (extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, neuroticism, and openness to experience) conducted by the Revived Genomics of Personality Consortium (ReGPC) across 46 cohorts comprising between 611,037 and 1.14 million participants with European-like and African-like genomes, alongside within-family GWAS in up to 50,725 (or 55,637) participants. The authors identify 1,260 independent lead genetic variants (including 824 novel loci), characterize common-variant SNP heritability ($4.8\text{--}9.3\%$, or $9.3\text{--}13.3\%$ at typical measurement reliability), and demonstrate strong generalizability of genetic architecture across geographic clusters, age groups, rater perspectives (self vs. close other), and measurement instruments. Furthermore, through dizygotic twin comparisons, parent-offspring trio decompositions, within-family GWAS, error-disattenuated assortative mating models, functional gene-set enrichment, polygenic index (PGI) prediction, and Mendelian randomization, the study demonstrates that genetic associations with personality are minimally confounded by shared family environments or spousal assortment, operate through broad neuronal gene sets under purifying selection, and exhibit widespread correlations and putative causal links with health, psychopathology, and sociodemographic outcomes.

## Strengths
* **Unprecedented Empirical Scale and Multi-Ancestry Discovery:** By expanding sample sizes to over 611,000 participants for every Big Five dimension and up to 1.14 million for neuroticism across 46 cohorts, the study crosses a critical statistical power threshold for all five traits simultaneously, achieving a 3- to 43-fold increase in genome-wide significant loci and establishing the first statistically significant out-of-sample polygenic prediction of personality in individuals with African-like genomes.
* **Rigorous Multi-Design Evaluation of Familial Confounding and Assortative Mating:** The authors triangulate dizygotic twin comparisons, parent-offspring trio decompositions, within-family GWAS across 12 cohorts, and disattenuated PGI structural equation modeling across over 320,000 family dyads to demonstrate that personality PGIs retain ~96% of their predictive power within families and exhibit minimal genetic assortative mating ($r_g = 0.01\text{--}0.04$), providing a crucial contrast to heavily confounded socioeconomic and cognitive traits.
* **Comprehensive Psychometric and Neurobiological Validation:** The integration of Genomic SEM across five major questionnaire families and self- versus informant-report comparisons in the Estonian Biobank ($r_g = 0.84$) convincingly establishes that genetic effects operate on broad latent constructs rather than self-report or instrument-specific artifacts, while single-cell and protein-truncating variant-intolerant gene-set enrichments offer compelling evidence for purifying selection and complex, overlapping neuronal architectures.
* **Broad Interdisciplinary Relevance to Biobehavioral and Social Outcomes:** The study maps extensive genetic correlations, polygenic predictions, and bidirectional Mendelian randomization links across 85 external phenotypes—including psychiatric nosology, diurnal physical activity, survey participation biases, and residential mobility—demonstrating the pervasive relevance of personality genetics across psychology, psychiatry, and epidemiology.

## Weaknesses
* **Nuances in Within-Family vs. Population-Level GWAS Concordance:** While within-family PGI analyses show ~96% preservation of predictive effects, the SNP-level within-family GWAS results exhibit wider confidence intervals, genetic correlations significantly below unity with population-level estimates for neuroticism ($r_g = 0.75$) and agreeableness ($r_g = 0.79$), and matched-cohort $h^2_{\text{SNP}}$ attenuation for openness to experience; the authors might consider expanding their discussion of how cohort-specific heterogeneity, differential effective weighting, and terminology (such as distinguishing regression coefficient ratios from proportions of explained variance $R^2$ in the parent-offspring decomposition) influence these comparisons.
* **Conditionality of Cross-Instrument Homogeneity ($Q_{\text{SNP}}$) on Post-Hoc Cross-Loadings:** The conclusion from Genomic SEM that only a single locus exhibits significant instrument heterogeneity ($Q_{\text{SNP}}$) is evaluated on a modified five-factor model that incorporates 11 iteratively added, data-driven cross-loadings (some moderate-to-large in magnitude); please consider explicitly noting in the main text how absorbing cross-trait instrument covariance into these post-hoc cross-loadings lowers $Q_{\text{SNP}}$ relative to the strictly confirmatory simple-structure model, and clarifying the exact statistical stopping rule used to select those cross-loadings.
* **Permissive Significance Heuristic and Potential Biases in Mendelian Randomization:** The bidirectional Mendelian randomization analysis evaluates over 130 exposure-outcome pairs without formal multiple-testing correction and relies on a "2 out of 3" significance rule under which 29 of the 33 putative causal pairs reach significance solely via clumped-SNP estimators (weighted median and weighted mode) while the 95% credible interval for MR-CAUSE—the only estimator explicitly modeling correlated horizontal pleiotropy and sample overlap (e.g., with UK Biobank outcomes)—includes zero; please consider highlighting the subset of findings supported by MR-CAUSE and discussing the impact of multiple testing and sample overlap.
* **Statistical Specification of Psychometric Disattenuation, Assortative Mating Scaling, and Transcriptomic Scope:** Several quantitative modeling choices would benefit from fuller justification or methodological detail, including the use of an unconstrained additive linear meta-regression weighted by $\text{s.e.}^{-1}$ to disattenuate $h^2_{\text{SNP}}$ (which implies non-zero heritability at zero scale reliability $\alpha = 0$ rather than a multiplicative proportional model passing through the origin), the unspecified algebraic formulas used to transform sibling and cousin PGI correlations onto the spousal scale (and account for split-half $r_g = 0.94 < 1$), and the need to temper conclusions regarding dopaminergic and serotonergic theories given the scope of adult post-mortem cell-body transcriptomic enrichment tests (Reference Paper: Neurobiology of the structure of personality: dopamine, facilitation of incentive motivation, and extraversion, Depue & Collins, 1999; Reference Paper: Handbook of Personality: Theory and Research, DeYoung et al., 2022).
* **Internal Inconsistencies in Sample Sizes, Citations, and Compiled Reporting Summary Encoding:** The manuscript contains several discrepancies across the text, tables, and figures—such as a factual reversal of EUR-like and AFR-like cohort counts in the trans-ancestry Methods ($k = 36$), conflicting previous lead SNP counts (14 vs. 17) and sample size sums for extraversion, differing within-family sample size upper bounds (50,725 vs. 55,637) and twin counts vs. families in NTR/TEDS, and severe font-encoding corruption across the compiled Nature Portfolio Reporting Summary (pages 27–29)—which the authors might consider reconciling to ensure seamless reproducibility.

### [Population-Level GWAS Meta-Analysis, SNP Heritability, Generalizability & Biological Annotation] (Pages: 2-4, 16-21)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Statistical Specification of the Psychometric Disattenuation Model for $h^2_{\text{SNP}}$ (Page 2, Table 1, and Page 17, Extended Data Fig. 2):**
   - **Observation:** In the "Characterizing SNP heritability" section (page 2), Table 1, and Extended Data Fig. 2 (page 17), measurement error in personality scales is accounted for by fitting an unconstrained additive linear multilevel meta-regression of cohort-specific $h^2_{\text{SNP}}$ against scale reliability (Cronbach's $\alpha$, centered at $1.0$ via $\alpha - 1$), weighted by the inverse standard error ($\text{s.e.}^{-1}$) of each $h^2_{\text{SNP}}$ estimate. This model yields a pooled linear slope of $b = 6.7\%$ ($\text{s.e.} = 0.7\%$) and generates the expected $h^2_{\text{SNP}}$ estimates reported in Table 1 at typical reliability ($\alpha = 0.81$: $9.3\text{--}13.3\%$) and perfect reliability ($\alpha = 1.0$: $10.8\text{--}15.8\%$).
   - **Potential Concern:** Under classical psychometric test theory (Spearman, 1910, ref. 35; Tucker-Drob, 2017, ref. 36), if a measured phenotype $Y_{\text{obs}} = Y_{\text{true}} + e$ has reliability $\alpha = \text{Var}(Y_{\text{true}}) / \text{Var}(Y_{\text{obs}})$ and random response error $e$ is uncorrelated with genotype, measurement error inflates total phenotypic variance without altering additive genetic covariance. Consequently, observed SNP heritability scales *multiplicatively* with reliability: $h^2_{\text{SNP, obs}} = \alpha \cdot h^2_{\text{SNP, true}}$ (or $h^2_{\text{SNP, true}} = h^2_{\text{SNP, obs}} / \alpha$), which enforces a proportional relationship passing through the origin ($h^2_{\text{SNP, obs}} = 0$ when $\alpha = 0$) with trait-specific slopes equal to $h^2_{\text{SNP, true}}$. Fitting an unconstrained additive linear model raises two related considerations:
     * *Implied heritability at zero reliability and weighting scheme:* Because the estimated linear slope ($6.7\%$) is substantially smaller than the predicted intercepts at $\alpha = 1.0$ ($10.8\%$ to $15.8\%$), the linear specification implies a non-zero SNP heritability of $4.1\%$ to $9.1\%$ when scale reliability is zero ($\alpha = 0$, i.e., $\alpha - 1 = -1.0$), which conflicts with the premise on page 2 that *"Random response error by the participants cannot systematically relate to their genome."* Moreover, because the meta-regression weights cohorts by inverse standard error ($\text{s.e.}^{-1}$, as noted in the Extended Data Fig. 2 caption) rather than standard random-effects inverse variance ($\text{s.e.}^{-2} + \hat{\tau}^2$), the predicted values at the median cohort reliability ($\alpha = 0.81$: $9.3\text{--}13.3\%$) sit noticeably above both the random-effects meta-analytic means ($7.4\text{--}10.6\%$ in Table 1) and the bulk of the high-precision biobank estimates in Extended Data Fig. 2.
     * *Confounding with scale length and construct breadth:* Across contributing cohorts, scale reliability $\alpha$ covaries strongly with inventory length and cohort design (e.g., ultra-brief 2- to 10-item scales in large biobanks versus comprehensive inventories such as the NEO-PI-R or 100NP in smaller cohorts). Because brief scales sample a narrower bandwidth of personality facets than longer inventories, an empirical cross-cohort regression of $h^2_{\text{SNP}}$ on $\alpha$ can conflate random response error with construct breadth and between-cohort genetic heterogeneity ($\tau = 3.6\%$). Clarifying the rationale for using an unconstrained additive linear meta-regression weighted by $\text{s.e.}^{-1}$—and comparing those predictions against classical multiplicative disattenuation ($h^2_{\text{SNP}, i} / \alpha_i$ or a zero-intercept proportional model)—would strengthen the quantitative rigor of the disattenuated estimates in Table 1.

### [Polygenic Prediction, Biobehavioral Correlates, Assortative Mating & Mendelian Randomization] (Pages: 4-7, 22-25)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Mendelian Randomization (MR) Significance Criteria, Correlated Horizontal Pleiotropy, and Sample Overlap (Page 7; Extended Data Fig. 9, Page 25):**
   - **Potential Concern Regarding Correlated Horizontal Pleiotropy Under the "2 out of 3" Rule:** In the bidirectional MR analyses across 5 personality traits and 13 selected biobehavioural outcomes (plus 3 negative controls), an exposure–outcome pairing is classified as a plausible causal effect if point estimates are directionally consistent across all three estimators (weighted median with Steiger filtering, weighted mode with Steiger filtering, and MR-CAUSE) and unadjusted 95% confidence or credible intervals exclude zero in at least two of the three estimators. A potential concern with this decision rule is that weighted median and weighted mode operate on the same set of clumped genome-wide significant lead SNPs and rely on majority-validity ($\ge 50\%$ valid weight) or zero-modal-pleiotropy (uncorrelated pleiotropy) assumptions, whereas MR-CAUSE is the only estimator among the three that uses genome-wide summary statistics to explicitly model correlated horizontal pleiotropy (where genetic variants affect both exposure and outcome via a shared heritable confounder). Under the "at least 2 out of 3" rule, an association is declared putatively causal when weighted median and weighted mode reach nominal significance ($P < 0.05$) even when MR-CAUSE's 95% credible interval spans zero. In Extended Data Fig. 9, 29 of the 33 reported pairings achieve significance solely through weighted median and weighted mode while MR-CAUSE spans zero (with MR-CAUSE excluding zero in only 4 pairings: `Extraversion -> COVID infection`, `Neuroticism -> Study participation: Mental Health`, `Neuroticism -> Study participation: IDK`, and `Openness -> Number of jobs held`), and in several instances the MR-CAUSE point estimate is attenuated by nearly an order of magnitude relative to the median/mode estimators (e.g., `Neuroticism -> Longevity`, where weighted mode is $-0.16$ and weighted median is $-0.14$, whereas MR-CAUSE is $-0.01 [-0.04, 0.02]$; or `Extraversion -> ADHD`, where weighted median is $0.36 [0.21, 0.51]$ and weighted mode is $0.44 [0.05, 0.84]$, whereas MR-CAUSE is $0.09 [-0.03, 0.21]$).
   - **Potential Concern Regarding Multiple Testing and Sample Overlap:** Evaluating 5 personality traits across 13 primary outcomes bidirectionally entails 130 primary directional tests (plus 30 negative-control tests), yet the significance criteria rely on unadjusted 95% intervals across two positively correlated clumped-instrument estimators without multiple-testing correction. Furthermore, large cohorts such as the UK Biobank contribute substantially to both the EUR-like Big Five discovery GWAS and several outcome GWAS (such as UK Biobank survey participation, "I don't know" responses, BMI, and spells in hospital). While MR-CAUSE explicitly estimates and accounts for sample overlap using genome-wide summary statistics, two-sample weighted median and weighted mode estimators can be biased toward the confounded observational correlation when sample overlap is substantial, which can also affect Steiger filtering. Explicitly distinguishing the subset of associations where MR-CAUSE also excludes zero (and passes the $\Delta\text{ELPD}$ model comparison against the sharing model), clarifying how multiple testing affects false-discovery expectations, and noting whether leave-UK-Biobank-out summary statistics were used for UK Biobank-derived outcomes would strengthen the causal inferences.

2. **Scaling, Split-Half Heterogeneity, and Interpretation of Historical Assortative Mating Models (Pages 5–7, 13, Fig. 2c; Extended Data Fig. 8, Page 24):**
   - **Potential Concern Regarding Sibling/Cousin Scaling and Split-Half Heterogeneity ($r_g < 1$):** To estimate historical genetic assortative mating ($r_g = 0.01\text{--}0.04$; Fig. 2c), the structural equation model in `lavaan` (Extended Data Fig. 8b) disattenuates split-half PGIs (PGI 1 and PGI 2) for measurement error by modelling the two PGIs as parallel indicators of a unit-variance latent genetic factor for each relative, and meta-analyses estimates across spousal, sibling, and cousin dyads after transforming sibling and cousin latent PGI correlations onto the spousal scale. Two methodological details warrant clarification for reproducibility and validity:
     1. Neither the main text (pages 5–7), the Methods section (page 13), nor the caption of Extended Data Fig. 8 specifies the exact quantitative genetic formula used to transform sibling and cousin latent PGI correlations ($r_{g,\text{sib}}$ and $r_{g,\text{cousin}}$, which have baseline expectations of $0.50$ and $0.125$ under random mating) onto the inferred spousal genetic correlation scale ($r_{g,\text{spouse}}$)—along with the accompanying transformation of their standard errors for inverse-variance weighting (e.g., whether a single-generation or multi-generational equilibrium parameterization such as $r_{g,\text{sib}} = \frac{1}{2}(1 + r_{g,\text{spouse}})$ was applied, and whether cousin correlations account for assortment across one or both parental marriages).
     2. While split-half disattenuation is unbiased when the two split-half discovery GWAS index the exact same genetic signal ($r_{g,\text{split}} = 1$), the main text reports that the two EUR-like split halves have a mean genetic correlation of $r_g = 0.94$ (page 2). When $r_{g,\text{split}} < 1$, the within-person covariance between PGI 1 and PGI 2 is attenuated by $r_{g,\text{split}}$, which can cause the latent variable model to underestimate PGI reliability and over-disattenuate the latent correlation between relatives by approximately $1/r_{g,\text{split}} \approx 1.064$. While multiplying a direct spousal correlation near $0.02$ by $1.064$ is negligible, scaling the baseline $0.50$ full-sibling correlation by $1/r_{g,\text{split}}$ prior to subtracting $0.50$ can introduce an upward bias in sibling-inferred parental assortment unless split-half heterogeneity is accounted for. Clarifying the algebraic scaling formula (or cross-referencing the exact Supplementary Note) and reporting how the direct spousal latent PGI correlations compare with the sibling- and cousin-derived estimates prior to meta-analysis would improve transparency.
   - **Theoretical Comparison Between Genetic ($r_g$) and Phenotypic ($r$) Mate Correlations:** On page 7, the inferred parental genetic correlations ($r_g = 0.01\text{--}0.04$; Fig. 2c) are contrasted directly with the phenotypic mate correlations in deCODE ($r = 0.07\text{--}0.12$ for E, A, C, N, and $r = 0.18$ for openness; Extended Data Fig. 8a), noting that phenotypic estimates *"were stronger in magnitude."* Under primary phenotypic assortative mating—where partners assort on the observed phenotype with correlation $r_{\text{pheno}}$—quantitative genetic theory predicts that the induced single-generation correlation between mates' standardized additive SNP factors is scaled by SNP heritability ($r_{g,\text{SNP}} \approx h^2_{\text{SNP}} \times r_{\text{pheno}}$). Given the disattenuated $h^2_{\text{SNP}}$ estimates of $0.09\text{--}0.16$ (Table 1), a phenotypic mate correlation of $r = 0.07\text{--}0.18$ directly implies an expected SNP-level partner correlation of roughly $0.01\text{--}0.03$. Clarifying this scaling relationship on page 7 would help readers see that $r_g = 0.01\text{--}0.04$ is quantitatively consistent with primary phenotypic assortment at the observed phenotypic correlations, whereas traits like educational attainment ($r_g = 0.37$) exceed $h^2_{\text{SNP}} \times r_{\text{pheno}}$ due to strong social homogamy or multi-generational equilibrium effects.

### [Within-Family PGI and GWAS Evaluation of Familial Confounding] (Pages: 7-9, 26)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

* **Terminological Distinction Between Effect-Size Ratios ($\beta$) and Proportions of Variance ($R^2$) in the Parent–Offspring PGI Decomposition (Page 7 and Page 8, Fig. 4b):**
  In the "Polygenic prediction within families" section on page 7, the text states that across the Big Five, *"direct effects accounted for an average of 96.2% of variance in population-level PGI prediction"* and that for educational attainment *"only 75.1% of variance was explained by direct effects."* However, as defined in the Fig. 4b legend on page 8 (*"The ratio of direct effects to direct plus confounding effects is shown at the bottom of the corresponding bar"*) and illustrated in the conscientiousness sentence on page 7 (*"explaining 8.1% of the total predicted effect, indirect effect $B = 0.015$"*, which complements the 92% direct ratio plotted for conscientiousness in Fig. 4b), the reported percentages (the mean of 104%, 95%, 92%, 95%, and 95% across the Big Five in Fig. 4b, and 75% for educational attainment) represent ratios of standardized regression coefficients ($\beta_{\text{direct}} / (\beta_{\text{direct}} + \beta_{\text{confound}})$) rather than proportions of explained variance ($R^2$). Because explained variance scales quadratically with regression coefficients and incorporates the covariance between transmitted and non-transmitted parental alleles—and because a ratio of signed regression coefficients can exceed 100% when the confounding estimate is slightly negative, as seen in the 104% value for extraversion in Fig. 4b—referring to 96.2% and 75.1% as proportions of *"variance"* is a potential concern that could confuse readers. Consistently referring to these quantities as proportions of the *total predicted effect* (or *population-level PGI association*) would ensure mathematical precision.

### [Statistical Genetics, Psychometric, and Causal Inference Methodology] (Pages: 12-14)

1. **Misstated Cohort Ancestry Composition in Trans-Ancestry Meta-Analysis (Page 12, 'Population-level meta-analysis'):**
   In the third paragraph of the 'Population-level meta-analysis' subsection, the text states: *"We next conducted transancestry IVW meta-analyses to synthesize data across the $k = 10$ cohorts that included participants with both EUR-like and AFR-like genomes, and the additional $k = 36$ cohorts including solely participants with AFR-like genomes."*

The additional $k = 36$ cohorts included solely participants with **EUR-like** genomes, not AFR-like genomes.

2. **Underspecified Estimators for Sample-Overlap Adjustment, Effective Sample Size Re-Estimation, and Trans-Ancestry Clumping (Pages 12–14, 'Population-level meta-analysis' and 'Evaluating familial confounding'):**
   Several analytical steps in the meta-analytic pipeline are described conceptually without stating the exact mathematical estimators or referencing the specific Supplementary Notes where they are derived:
   - *Sample-overlap error dependency (Page 12):* The text states that for potentially overlapping cohorts, *"we meta-analysed estimates while accounting for dependency of their estimation errors,"* without specifying the estimator used to quantify and adjust for the error covariance across overlapping cohorts (e.g., whether bivariate LDSC intercepts were used to construct a sampling covariance matrix for generalized inverse-variance weighting).
   - *Effective sample size ($n$) re-estimation (Pages 12 and 13–14):* The text states that per-SNP $n$ was re-estimated from meta-analytic standard errors and MAF (using observed meta-analytic MAF in the primary population-level GWAS, and 1000 Genomes 3v5 MAF capped at the 75th percentile of summed observed $n$ in the within-family and matched 12-cohort population-level GWAS). Because within-family estimators (`snipar` and within-sibship regression) operate on Mendelian segregation or sibling differences whose genotypic variance is half that of unrelated individuals in the population, the algebraic relationship between $\text{s.e.}$, $\text{MAF}$, and effective $n$ differs between within-family and population-level designs. Explicitly providing the back-calculation equations for both designs (and clarifying why reference panel MAF and the 75th-percentile cap were applied to the matched population-level GWAS but not the full population-level GWAS) would prevent ambiguity.
   - *Trans-ancestry LD matrix construction (Page 12):* When constructing *"transancestry sample size-weighted LD matrices for each chromosome"* from the 1000 Genomes 3v5 EUR and AFR reference panels to identify additional LD-independent lead SNPs, the text does not specify whether sample-size weighting was applied to signed allelic correlations ($r$) or squared correlations ($r^2$), nor the $r^2$ threshold and genomic window size applied during this secondary clumping step.

3. **Specification of the Parent–Offspring PGI Decomposition (Page 13, 'Evaluating familial confounding'):**
   In describing the deCODE parent–offspring analysis, the text states that an offspring's personality trait is predicted from their PGI controlling for their mother's and father's PGIs, and that *"we obtained estimates of the population-level effect (the total prediction) and the indirect genetic confounding (the contribution to prediction made by parental PGI scores, both together and separately by mother and father), and we estimated the direct genetic effect by subtracting the indirect genetic confounding from the population-level effect."* A potential concern for clarity is that in a multiple regression of offspring phenotype on proband PGI controlling for maternal and paternal PGIs, the partial regression coefficient on the proband's PGI directly estimates the direct genetic effect (consistent with the Fig. 4b caption: *"directly transmitted effects (effects of the proband's PGI; darker bars) and familial confounds (effects of the proband's parents' PGIs; lighter bars)"*), whereas the population-level effect is either estimated from an unconditional regression omitting parental PGIs or reconstructed as a linear combination of the direct and parental coefficients. Stating the exact regression specifications and algebraic definitions used to define these three quantities would remove this ambiguity.
