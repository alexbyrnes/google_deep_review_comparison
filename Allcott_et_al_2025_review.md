# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY
---
## Summary
This paper reports the results of two large-scale randomized controlled trials evaluating the causal effect of deactivating Facebook and Instagram on users' self-reported emotional state during the six weeks preceding the 2020 U.S. presidential election. Recruiting 19,857 Facebook users and 15,585 Instagram users, the authors find that an incremental five weeks of pre-election deactivation improved an index of happiness, depression, and anxiety by 0.060 standard deviations on Facebook and 0.041 standard deviations on Instagram relative to a one-week deactivation control group. Furthermore, exploratory subgroup analyses indicate that the emotional benefits of deactivation are concentrated among adults over 35 on Facebook and women under 25 on Instagram, while passive smartphone tracking reveals that nearly all time freed by deactivation is substituted toward other mobile applications rather than offline activities.

## Strengths
* **Unprecedented Experimental Scale and Platform Isolation:** The study is roughly 20 times larger than the largest prior social media abstention trials and enforces a longer five-week incremental abstention period verified directly through internal platform logs. Additionally, it is the first randomized field experiment to isolate the causal impact of Instagram deactivation on emotional state separately from Facebook.
* **High-Powered Demographic Subgroup Discovery:** Because of its exceptional statistical power, the experiment is able to detect important demographic heterogeneity that smaller trials could not identify, providing large-scale experimental evidence that Facebook's negative emotional effects are driven by users over 35 while Instagram's effects are strongly concentrated among young women aged 18–24.
* **Objective Telemetry on Cross-App Substitution:** By combining platform-enforced account deactivation with passive smartphone tracking, the paper demonstrates that approximately three-quarters of freed Facebook time and virtually all freed Instagram time are reallocated to other mobile applications, overturning earlier self-reported findings and clarifying that emotional state improvements stem from differences in platform user experiences rather than increased offline time.
* **Rigorous Pre-Registration and Contextual Benchmarking:** The empirical strategy adheres to a detailed pre-analysis plan, contextualizes effect magnitudes against six informative benchmarks (including clinical psychological interventions, partisan baselines, and national youth mental health trends), and demonstrates how standard non-experimental correlation and panel designs yield biased estimates in the same dataset.

## Weaknesses
* **Potential Exclusion Restriction and Identification Nuances in the IV Framework:** The instrumental variables estimation bundles the five-week incremental deactivation requirement with an additional $125 financial payment ($150 versus $25 in the Control group) and relies on an extensive-margin daily login threshold ($D_i$) rather than intensive-margin usage minutes; the authors might consider expanding their discussion of why the payment differential, intensive-margin adjustments, or potential post-week-1 rebound effects in the active Control group do not violate the exclusion restriction.
* **Temporal Overlap Between the Survey Recall Window and Post-Reactivation Usage:** Because accounts were automatically reactivated on November 4 while the endline survey was fielded from November 4 to November 18 using a "past four weeks" recall window, late responders reported on a period partially diluted by up to two weeks of post-reactivation platform access, which also coincided with differential response timing around the November 7 election call.
* **Different Age Cutoffs Across Platforms in Subgroup Comparisons and Unreported Pooled Benchmarks:** In the exploratory age-by-gender analysis, using sample-specific median splits defines the younger cohort as 18–34 on Facebook versus 18–24 on Instagram, which could obscure effects among 18–24-year-olds on Facebook; similarly, in Section 4.4, the sixth benchmark compares Instagram's effect on 18–24-year-olds to historical Kessler-6 trends without explicitly reporting the pooled 18–24 Instagram point estimate in the main text.
* **Contextualization of Prior Passive-Tracking Literature and Minor Quantitative Transpositions:** While the paper is the first to combine internal platform deactivation logs with passive mobile tracking, the authors might consider briefly noting how their substitution findings complement prior smaller-scale passive-tracking restriction trials (Reference Paper: Effects of Restricting Social Media Usage on Wellbeing and Performance: A Randomized Control Trial Among Students, Avinash Collis and Felix Eggers, 2022) and how the design and telemetry build on their foundational midterm and companion political-outcomes studies (Reference Paper: The Welfare Effects of Social Media, Hunt Allcott et al., 2020; Reference Paper: The Effects of Facebook and Instagram on the 2020 Election: A Deactivation Experiment, Hunt Allcott et al., 2024). Additionally, in Section 4.4, the percentage benchmarks comparing Facebook (0.060 SD) and Instagram (0.041 SD) to the average psychological intervention (0.27 SD in van Agteren et al., 2021) appear inadvertently transposed ("15 and 22 percent" instead of 22 and 15 percent, respectively) (Reference Paper: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Psychological Interventions to Improve Mental Wellbeing, Joep van Agteren et al., 2021).

### [Experimental Design, Descriptive Statistics, and Empirical Strategy] (Pages: 10-17)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Temporal Mismatch Between the Four-Week Survey Recall Window, the Active Treatment Period, and the Post-Election Call (Sections 1, 1.1, 2, and 3, pages 10–11, 15–17):**
   There is a potential concern regarding the alignment between the five-week active differential treatment period (September 30–November 3), the 15-day endline survey fielding window (November 4–18), and the four-week retrospective recall window of the emotional state outcome measures:
   - *Post-Reactivation Dilution in the Recall Window:* In Section 1 (page 10), Deactivation group accounts were automatically reactivated on November 4, while NORC fielded the endline survey from November 4 to November 18. Because the emotional state items ask how much of the time "during the past four weeks" respondents felt happy, depressed, or anxious (Section 1.1, page 11), participants who completed the endline survey late in the window (e.g., around November 18) reported on a four-week period consisting of only two weeks of active deactivation (October 21–November 4) and up to two full weeks *after* their accounts had already been reactivated (November 4–18). Meanwhile, in Section 3 (page 17), the endogenous compliance variable $D_i = 1 - U_i / \bar{U}_C$ in Equation (1) is defined strictly over the five-week September 30–November 3 treatment period rather than the respondent-specific four-week window preceding their endline survey completion date. This partial overlap between the outcome recall period and post-reactivation usage introduces potential treatment dilution into the local average treatment effect (LATE) estimate $\tau$.
   - *Interaction of Differential Response Timing with the Election Call:* Section 2 documents three empirical regularities: (i) the Deactivation group completed the endline survey systematically earlier than the Control group (page 15), (ii) the study sample has a higher proportion of users with liberal views than the general platform populations (page 15), and (iii) the partisan well-being gap between Democrats and Republicans narrowed among respondents who completed the endline survey after major media outlets called the election for Biden (pages 16–17, which occurred on November 7). Because the four-week recall window shifts with the survey completion date, earlier response timing by the Deactivation group implies that Deactivation respondents not only had greater overlap with the active pre-November 4 deactivation period, but also that a larger share of Deactivation respondents answered prior to the November 7 election call compared to Control respondents. While Section 2 notes that response timing is assessed later in the paper (Section 4.5), explicitly discussing in Sections 2 and 3 how post-November 4 reactivation dilutes the recall window for late responders and whether differential response timing across the November 7 cutoff interacts with party identification would strengthen the empirical strategy.

2. **Potential Exclusion Restriction Concerns in the Instrumental Variables Specification (Sections 1 and 3, pages 10–11, 17):**
   In Section 3 (page 17), the instrumental variables (IV) regression in Equation (1), $Y_i = \tau D_i + \rho \boldsymbol{X}_i + \nu_s + \varepsilon_i$, uses the Deactivation group assignment indicator $T_i$ to instrument for deactivation compliance $D_i = 1 - U_i / \bar{U}_C$ during the September 30–November 3 window. Identifying $\tau$ as the causal effect of platform abstention requires the exclusion restriction that assignment $T_i$ affects endline emotional state $Y_i$ solely through $D_i$. Several features of the experimental design in Sections 1 and 3 represent potential concerns for this assumption that warrant explicit discussion:
   - *Differential Financial Compensation (\$150 vs. \$25):* As stated in Section 1 (page 10), "the only differences between Control and Deactivation were the total payment amount and deactivation length," with compliant Deactivation participants promised \$150 (for six weeks) and compliant Control participants promised \$25 (for one week). If receiving or anticipating an additional \$125 transfer around the time of the endline survey directly improves self-reported happiness or reduces financial anxiety—or induces reciprocity/experimenter demand proportional to payment size (or conversely, a negative hedonic shock among the 15–20% of non-compliant Deactivation participants reminded on page 11 that they lost their \$150 payment)—$T_i$ would affect $Y_i$ independently of $D_i$. Given the modest magnitude of the estimated treatment effects (0.041 to 0.060 standard deviations), clarifying when deactivation payments were disbursed relative to endline survey completion and discussing why a \$125 payment differential is unlikely to drive the observed emotional state improvements would bolster the identification argument.
   - *Extensive vs. Intensive Margin of Usage:* Because $U_i$ is defined using a binary daily threshold (the share of days seeing five or more pieces of content, page 17), $D_i$ captures only the extensive margin of daily logins rather than the intensive margin of daily time spent on the platform. If $T_i$ also caused non-compliant or partially compliant participants to reduce their daily minutes on the days they did view five or more pieces of content (or to log in briefly and view fewer than five items), $T_i$ would affect $Y_i$ through an intensive-margin channel not captured by $D_i$. Additionally, because Equation (1) is also applied to the passive tracking subsample to estimate effects on daily minutes of mobile app usage (Section 4.2), clarifying in Section 3 how scaling daily minutes of app usage by the extensive-margin compliance share $D_i$ should be interpreted would improve clarity.
   - *Active Control Group Design and Post-Deactivation Rebound Dynamics:* Paying the Control group \$25 to deactivate for Week 1 (September 23–30) before automatically reactivating their accounts on September 30 (page 10) equalizes initial deactivation artifacts and reduces experimenter demand effects, but assumes that Control users' platform usage and emotional state during Weeks 2–6 represent an unperturbed counterfactual. If a one-week forced abstention followed by automatic reactivation right as the October campaign intensified induced a temporary rebound surge in usage or acute reentry stress in the Control group after September 30, this could affect the baseline comparison captured by $\tau$.

3. **Cross-Platform Comparability of Exploratory Age-by-Gender Subgroup Definitions (Section 1.2, page 13):**
   In Section 1.2 (page 13), the first exploratory moderator interacts gender with above- versus below-median age, allocating the coarse survey age category containing the median to the above-median group. Because the age distributions of the two samples differ, this median split yields age groups of **18–34 vs. 35+** for Facebook and **18–24 vs. 25+** for Instagram. A potential concern when comparing age-by-gender heterogeneity across the two experiments (such as the finding that Facebook effects are driven by people over 35 while Instagram effects are driven by women under 25) is that platform differences are confounded with a decade difference in the age cutoffs: users aged 25–34 are classified in the "younger" (below-median) subgroup on Facebook, where they are pooled with 18–24-year-olds, but in the "older" (above-median) subgroup on Instagram. Consequently, an effect concentrated specifically among 18–24-year-old women on Facebook could be diluted by pooling them with 25–34-year-old women. Since the underlying survey instrument is identical across both experiments and distinguishes 18–24 from 25–34, clarifying in Section 1.2 whether the results hold under harmonized age bins (e.g., evaluating 18–24, 25–34, and 35+ across both platforms) would help readers separate true platform-specific heterogeneity from artifacts of the differing age cutoffs.

4. **Reproducibility Details for LASSO Covariate Selection, Baseline Imbalance Mitigation, Subsample Weighting, and Outcome Standardization (Sections 1.1, 2, and 3, pages 11, 16–17):**
   Several methodological details in Sections 1.1, 2, and 3 would benefit from brief clarification to ensure reproducibility and verify bias mitigation:
   - *Guaranteed Inclusion of Baseline Emotional State Under LASSO:* Section 2 (page 16) reports that in the Facebook sample, the Deactivation group had a statistically significantly worse baseline emotional state by chance, and states: *"As pre-specified, our regressions control for baseline emotional state, which mitigates that bias."* Section 3 (page 17) states: *"As pre-specified, the control variables $\boldsymbol{X}_i$ are those selected by a lasso regression of the endline outcome $Y_i$ on its baseline value plus a set of demographic variables and baseline political beliefs and behaviors."* In standard penalized LASSO regressions, candidate covariates are subject to shrinkage and selection, meaning the baseline outcome variable is not guaranteed to be retained unless it is left unpenalized (forced into the active set). Explicitly confirming in Section 3 whether the baseline outcome was forced into $\boldsymbol{X}_i$ unpenalized across all primary, component, and subgroup regressions—as well as how the LASSO penalty was tuned, whether sample weights were applied during selection, and why single-equation outcome selection was used rather than post-double-selection across $Y_i$, $D_i$, and $T_i$—would assure readers that the Bruhn and McKenzie (2009) correction for baseline imbalance is guaranteed in every specification.
   - *Post-Randomization Conditioning and Weighting in the Passive Tracking Subsample:* Section 2 (page 16) states that passive tracking analyses are limited to the 29 percent of Facebook participants and 25 percent of Instagram participants who had at least two days of baseline data before September 23 and *"non-missing data on at least 85 percent of days both during their baseline period and in the post-intervention period between September 23 and November 4."* Conditioning sample inclusion on maintaining at least 85 percent non-missing tracking days during the September 23–November 4 post-intervention period conditions on a post-randomization behavior (though overall inclusion rates are balanced between Deactivation and Control groups). Furthermore, while Section 3 (page 17) states that *"all analyses below weight observations to make the sample representative of focal platform users with baseline use greater than 15 minutes per day,"* the text does not specify whether the 25–29 percent of participants who opted into passive tracking differ systematically from the primary analysis sample on baseline observables, nor whether the passive tracking analyses reuse the main survey weights or use re-estimated weights that adjust for selection into the passive tracking subsample.
   - *Reference Distribution for Outcome Standardization:* Section 1.1 (page 11) states that the three emotional state variables are standardized *"to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one in the sample-weighted combined Control groups,"* and their average (*emotional state index*) is then *"re-standardized to have a standard deviation of one in the sample-weighted combined Control groups."* However, the text does not specify whether "combined Control groups" refers to pooling the Facebook and Instagram Control groups together, nor whether the standardization is anchored to a single survey wave (e.g., endline or baseline) or performed separately within each wave (noting that Section 2, page 16 compares Control group emotional state levels across baseline, endline, and December waves, which implies the Control mean could not have been set to zero independently in every wave). Explicitly stating the exact reference distribution in Section 1.1 would ensure reproducibility.

### [Impact Evaluation Results, Subgroup Heterogeneity, Benchmarks, and Conclusion] (Pages: 18-27)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

* **Verification Summary:** The econometric and quantitative analyses in Sections 4 and 5 (PDF pages 18–27)—including the first-stage compliance rates and coefficients ($0.871$ for Facebook and $0.893$ for Instagram), the passive-tracking app substitution accounting in Figure 1 (where the $-37$ and $-20\text{ min/day}$ reductions in focal platform use and increases across the remaining mutually exclusive app categories sum consistently to the $-9\text{ min/day}$ and near-zero `All apps` totals), the main and subgroup local average treatment effects in Figure 2, the normal percentile conversions ($\Phi(0.060) \approx 52.4\%$ and $\Phi(0.041) \approx 51.6\%$), the raw $0\text{–}4$ unit conversions, the literature comparison meta-analysis in Figure 3 (summing to $17,802 + 13,480 = 31,282$ endline completers), and the attrition and response-timing robustness checks in Section 4.5—were verified and are internally consistent and methodologically sound. The points below highlight specific areas where quantitative benchmarks appear transposed or where statistical reporting could be clarified.

1. **Transposed Percentage Benchmarks Relative to Psychological Interventions (Section 4.4, PDF Pages 23–24 / Manuscript Pages 21–22):**
   In the fifth benchmark contextualizing effect sizes, the text compares the estimated treatment effects to the van Agteren et al. (2021) meta-analysis average improvement of $0.27$ standard deviations, stating:
   > *"The average across the nine intervention types is 0.27 standard deviations. Thus, the point estimates imply that pre-election Facebook and Instagram deactivation, respectively, improved emotional state by 15 and 22 percent as much as the average psychological intervention."*

   This is a potential concern because the percentage figures for Facebook and Instagram are transposed relative to the *"Facebook and Instagram deactivation, respectively"* ordering. Using the reported local average treatment effects on the `emotional state index` from Section 4.3 ($0.060$ standard deviations for Facebook and $0.041$ standard deviations for Instagram, PDF page 20), dividing by the $0.27$ standard deviation benchmark yields:
   - **Facebook:** $0.060 / 0.27 = 22.2\%$ (approximately $22$ percent)
   - **Instagram:** $0.041 / 0.27 = 15.2\%$ (approximately $15$ percent)

   Reversing the order in the text to read *"by 22 and 15 percent as much as the average psychological intervention"* will align the calculation with the larger point estimate observed for Facebook.

2. **Unspecified Subgroup Point Estimate in the Sixth Benchmark Calculation (Section 4.4, PDF Page 24 / Manuscript Page 22):**
   In the sixth benchmark comparing the experimental effects to historical trends in the National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH), the text states:
   > *"From 2008 to 2022, Kessler-6 scores worsened by 0.37 standard deviations for people aged 18-24. The estimated effect of Instagram deactivation on people aged 18-24 is about 17 percent as large as this time-series change."*

   A potential concern for clarity and reproducibility is that the pooled point estimate for all Instagram users aged 18–24 (combining men and women) is not explicitly reported in the main text or displayed directly in Figure 2. A ratio of $17\%$ of $0.37$ standard deviations implies an underlying pooled treatment effect estimate of approximately $0.063$ standard deviations ($0.063 / 0.37 \approx 17.0\%$) for the `18-24` age group on Instagram. However, Section 4.3 and Panel B of Figure 2 (PDF pages 21–22) only disaggregate the 18–24 age group by gender—reporting separate estimates for `Female, aged 18-24 (IG)` ($0.111$ standard deviations, which would be $0.111 / 0.37 = 30.0\%$ of the time-series trend) and `Male, aged 18-24 (IG)` (visually near $0.01\text{–}0.02$ standard deviations)—while the overall Instagram sample average across all ages is $0.041$ standard deviations ($11.1\%$ of the trend). Explicitly stating the pooled `18-24` Instagram point estimate in parentheses in Section 4.4 (or cross-referencing the specific appendix table where the non-interacted age subgroup estimate is tabulated) would allow readers to verify the $17\%$ benchmark directly.

3. **Multiple Hypothesis Testing Context and Subgroup Comparison Wording in Exploratory Subgroup Analyses (Section 4.3, PDF Pages 20–22 / Manuscript Pages 18–20):**
   While the primary `emotional state index` is evaluated against both unadjusted $p$-values and sharpened False Discovery Rate (FDR) $q$-values adjusted across all 61 primary and secondary outcomes (yielding $q = 0.002$ for Facebook and $q = 0.139$ for Instagram), the non-preregistered exploratory subgroup analyses in Section 4.3 evaluate heterogeneity across five moderator dimensions in Figure 2 (plus additional demographic moderators referenced from Appendix D.4) for both platforms using unadjusted $p$-values. This raises two potential considerations regarding precision and statistical framing:
   - **Multiple Testing and Marginal Significance:** Several highlighted subgroup differences sit near or above the nominal $0.05$ threshold prior to any adjustment for testing multiple moderators—specifically, the Facebook age split ($p = 0.046$ for 35+ vs. 18–34), the omnibus test of equality across the four age-by-gender subgroups on Instagram ($p = 0.062$, which exceeds the $0.05$ threshold even before adjustment), undecided voters ($p = 0.053$), and users without college degrees ($p = 0.058$). While Section 1.2 transparently notes that the subgroup analyses are exploratory and report unadjusted $p$-values, briefly noting in Section 4.3 whether the highlighted subgroup findings (such as the $p = 0.002$ within-subgroup effect for young women on Instagram vs. the $p = 0.046$ age difference on Facebook) survive multiple-testing adjustments across the set of evaluated moderators would strengthen the statistical calibration of the discussion.
   - **Ambiguity in Subgroup Comparison Wording and Platform Specification (PDF Page 22):** In the final paragraph of Section 4.3, the text states: *"Appendix D.4 suggests possible heterogeneity along two other moderators: the point estimates are larger for undecided voters ($p = 0.053$ in a test of equality with non-undecided) and for people without college degrees ($p = 0.058$ in a test of equality with non-college)."* First, because *"people without college degrees"* and *"non-college"* refer to the same subgroup, *"in a test of equality with non-college"* appears misstated and should read *"in a test of equality with college graduates"* (or *"with college"*). Second, while the introduction (PDF page 6) notes that these two subgroup patterns apply to Facebook, the sentence on PDF page 22 does not specify which platform (Facebook or Instagram) these estimates and $p$-values correspond to. Explicitly naming the Facebook sample here will prevent ambiguity.

4. **Clarifying Point Estimates vs. Confidence Intervals for Lee (2009) Trimming Bounds (Section 4.5, PDF Page 27 / Manuscript Page 25):**
   In the robustness checks addressing the roughly 2 percentage point differential attrition rate between the Deactivation and Control groups, the text states:
   > *"Lee (2009) bounds exclude zero for happy and emotional state index in the Facebook experiment and for happy in the Instagram experiment, and they rule out negative effects of larger than 0.010 to 0.025 standard deviations for the other five outcomes."*

   A potential concern for statistical interpretation is that the text does not specify whether *"exclude zero"* and *"rule out negative effects"* refer to the **point estimates** of the Lee lower bounds or to the lower limits of their **confidence intervals** (e.g., Imbens and Manski, 2004). Given that the unadjusted $p$-values for several components (`anxious x (-1)` and `depressed x (-1)`) are between $0.110$ and $0.205$ even before trimming, clarifying in the text whether these statements describe the bound point estimates or their confidence intervals would help readers accurately assess the sensitivity of the results to differential attrition.
